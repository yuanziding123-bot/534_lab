What is a Treasury yield?
The yield is the annual return an investor locks in by buying a U.S. government bond today and holding it to maturity. Because Treasuries are treated as free of default risk, their yields are the benchmark that mortgages, corporate loans and stock valuations are priced against.
How to read this chart
Short maturities (3 months, 2 years) mostly track where markets expect the Federal Reserve to set rates. Long maturities (10, 30 years) also reflect growth, inflation and the extra pay investors demand for locking money up longer.

Term spreads
The gap between long and short yields measures the slope of the curve. When the 10-year falls below the 2-year or 3-month yield, the curve is inverted: markets expect rate cuts ahead, and inversions have preceded every U.S. recession since the 1970s.
Credit spreads
Option-adjusted spreads (ICE BofA indices) show how much extra yield corporate borrowers pay over Treasuries. They widen when investors fear defaults, so they are a quick read on risk appetite.

Realized inflation
CPI inflation is the year-over-year change in consumer prices measured by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Core excludes food and energy, which are volatile, so it is a better guide to the underlying trend.
Expected inflation
The breakeven rate is the nominal Treasury yield minus the TIPS (inflation-protected) yield. It is the inflation rate at which the two bonds would pay the same, so it is the market's own forecast. The nominal yield minus breakeven is the real yield.

Why scenarios?
Bond prices move opposite to yields, and longer bonds move more. A scenario applies a hypothetical shock to today's curve and reprices a par bond at each maturity, so you can see who wins and who loses.
The named shapes
Bear means yields rise (prices fall), bull means yields fall. Steepener means the long end moves up more (or down less) than the short end; flattener is the reverse.

Why run a simulation at all?
Nobody knows where yields will be in six months. A single forecast hides that uncertainty. A Monte Carlo simulation does the opposite: it generates thousands of possible futures, each one a random path that behaves the way the yield has behaved in the past. Looking at all of them together shows the range of outcomes and lets us attach rough probabilities to them, including to the scenarios on the previous tab.
How it works, in four steps
  1. Learn from history. Measure how much the yield typically moves in a day, and whether it tends to drift back toward a long-run level.
  2. Roll the dice. Starting from today's yield, add a random daily move, day after day, to build one path.
  3. Repeat thousands of times. Each repeat is one possible future.
  4. Count. The share of paths where something happens is our estimate of its probability.
